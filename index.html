Section 01

What is Artificial Intelligence?

AI is any computer system that performs tasks normally requiring human intelligence — understanding language, recognizing patterns, making decisions, solving problems. It is not magic; it is math at massive scale.

🧠 Simple Analogy: AI is like a very well-read intern who has absorbed millions of books, emails, and conversations. They can answer questions, write content, and assist with tasks — incredibly fast, but without genuine understanding. They pattern-match at superhuman speed.
Narrow AI
Specialist — One Task
Excels at exactly one thing.

Examples: Gmail spam filter, Netflix recommendations, Spotify's Discover Weekly, face unlock, Google Maps routing.
General AI (Today)
Multi-domain Assistant
Handles many different tasks — write, reason, code, analyze. Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini sit here today. Broad but not omniscient.
Super AI
Theoretical / Future
Surpasses human intelligence across all domains. Does not exist yet. A topic of intense research and ethical debate.

The AI Family Tree — How It All Nests

AI

Artificial Intelligence (AI) — The Big Tent

Any technique making computers mimic human intelligence. Everything below is a subset.

ML

Machine Learning (ML) ⊂ AI

Instead of hand-coded rules, the system learns patterns from data. Email spam detection learns from millions of spam examples — you never wrote a rule. It found the rules itself.

DL

Deep Learning (DL) ⊂ ML

Uses layered neural networks loosely inspired by the brain. Dramatically better at images, audio, and text than earlier ML. Powers virtually all modern AI breakthroughs.

LLM

Large Language Models (LLMs) ⊂ DL

Deep learning applied specifically to language — trained on vast text corpora to predict and generate text. The engine behind Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Llama.

Section 02

Large Language Models (LLMs)

An LLM is trained on a massive amount of text — books, web pages, code, scientific papers — so it learns the statistical patterns of human language and can generate, understand, and reason with text at a high level.

📚 Simple Analogy: Imagine you read every book ever written, every website ever published, and every conversation ever recorded — and then could answer questions, write essays, and help solve problems. That's what an LLM does. It has compressed all that reading into billions of numbers (weights) inside a neural network.

✅ What LLMs Excel At
  • Writing, summarizing, and translating text
  • Answering questions from their training knowledge
  • Generating and explaining code
  • Step-by-step reasoning and analysis
  • Classifying, extracting, and structuring data
  • Creative content — stories, emails, scripts
⚠️ Where LLMs Fall Short
  • Events after their training cutoff date
  • Live data — prices, stock, inventory, news
  • Precise arithmetic with large numbers
  • Persistent memory across sessions (without RAG)
  • Taking real-world actions (without Agent tools)
  • Guaranteed accuracy — they can hallucinate

How a Model is Created — The Training Lifecycle

Pre-training (Weeks, Millions of $)
Train on trillions of tokens from the internet, books, and code. The model learns language, facts, and reasoning from scratch. This creates the "base model" — it can predict text but isn't yet helpful.
Instruction Fine-tuning (Days)
Train on curated question-answer pairs to teach the model to follow instructions rather than just predict the next word. Makes it actually useful as an assistant.
RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (Days)
Human raters score model responses. A reward model learns what humans prefer. The LLM is fine-tuned to maximize that reward. This is why Claude is polite, helpful, and refuses harmful requests.
Deployment + Continuous Improvement
Model is served via API at scale. Real usage data informs future training iterations. Models improve version by version.
Section 04

Datasets — The Fuel for AI

A dataset is organized data used to train or fine-tune an AI model. Quality and quantity of data directly determine model capability. Garbage in, garbage out — applies doubly to AI.

🍳 Analogy: Training an AI is like teaching a chef. The dataset is the recipe collection — the more diverse and high-quality the recipes, the better the chef. A chef trained only on Italian cuisine will struggle when asked to make sushi. A model trained only on English will struggle with French.
Pre-training Data
The Giant Foundation Dataset
Huge and general — web crawls, books, code, academic papers. Teaches language, world knowledge, and reasoning. Done once. Costs tens of millions of dollars. Creates the base model.
Examples: Common Crawl (web), Wikipedia, GitHub, ArXiv, Books3, StackOverflow. GPT-4 was trained on an estimated 13 trillion tokens.
Fine-tuning Data
The Specialist Dataset
Smaller, curated dataset for a specific domain, task, or behavior. Far cheaper than pre-training. Customizes the base model into a domain expert.
Example: 10,000 Salesforce support conversations → fine-tune Llama to become a Salesforce support specialist that knows your exact product.

Data Quality Dimensions — What Makes a Good Dataset

🎯
Accuracy
Factual correctness. Wrong data trains wrong beliefs.
🌈
Diversity
Covers many topics, styles, and perspectives. Avoids bias.
📏
Scale
More data = more generalizable. LLMs need trillions of tokens.
🧹
Cleanliness
Deduplication, noise removal, encoding fixes. Raw web data is messy.
Section 05

RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation

RAG gives an LLM access to a live, searchable knowledge base so it can look up relevant information before answering — instead of relying only on what it memorized months ago during training.

📖 Analogy: A doctor can answer from memory (risky — might be outdated) or look it up in the medical database first, then answer. RAG is the "look it up first" approach. The LLM's answer is grounded in real, current documents — not hallucinated from stale training memory.

The RAG Pipeline — Full Architecture

①

📚 Ingest & Chunk Documents (done once, offline)

Your documents — PDFs, Word files, Confluence pages, Salesforce records, Notion pages — are split into small, overlapping chunks of 300–500 words each. Chunk size is a tuning parameter: smaller = more precise retrieval; larger = more context per result.

Example: A 200-page product manual → ~400 chunks of 3–4 paragraphs. A 50-page contract → ~80 chunks by clause.
②

🔢 Embed Chunks → Store in Vector DB (offline)

Each chunk is passed through an embedding model (e.g. OpenAI text-embedding-3-large) which converts it into a vector — a list of ~1536 floating-point numbers representing its semantic meaning. These vectors are stored in a vector database: Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector, Vertex AI Search, or Chroma.

Example: "How to reset password" → [0.23, -0.87, 0.04, 0.61, …1536 numbers] stored with a pointer back to the original text chunk.
③

❓ User Asks a Question (real-time)

The user's query is also embedded into a vector using the same embedding model. This query vector will be compared against all stored document chunk vectors.

Example: "How do I reset my BlackLine account password?" → embedded to a query vector.
④

🔍 Semantic Search — Find Closest Chunks

The vector DB computes cosine similarity between the query vector and all stored chunk vectors. The top-k (e.g., top 5) most similar chunks are retrieved. This is NOT keyword matching — it understands meaning. "password reset" and "account credentials recovery" are semantically close, so both return even without exact word matches.

⑤

📋 Build Augmented Prompt

Retrieved chunks are injected into the LLM's system prompt: [CONTEXT]: {chunk1} {chunk2} {chunk3} ... [USER QUESTION]: How do I reset my BlackLine password? The LLM is instructed to answer only from the provided context.

⑥

💬 LLM Generates a Grounded Answer

The LLM answers using your retrieved documents as evidence — grounded in real content, not hallucinated from training. Optionally, it can cite which document chunk the answer came from for full auditability.

Section 06

Skills — What Agents Can Do

A skill (also called a "tool" or "function") is a defined capability registered with an AI agent — a function the agent can call to interact with the real world beyond pure text generation.

📱 Analogy: Skills are like apps on a smartphone. Your phone (the agent) is intelligent, but without apps it can't do much practically. The Gmail app is the "email skill." Maps is the "navigation skill." Calendar is the "scheduling skill." You install the apps you need; the agent calls the skills it needs.
🔍

Search Skill

Search the web, a vector DB, or internal systems. Used when the agent needs information it doesn't have.

Example: "Find the latest Salesforce CPQ pricing" → calls web_search()
📧

Email Skill

Read, draft, and send emails through Gmail or Outlook. Agent uses this when communication is required.

Example: "Draft a follow-up to the prospect" → calls gmail_draft()
📊

CRM / Data Skill

Query or update Salesforce, databases, or spreadsheets. The agent reads and writes live business data.

Example: "Get open opportunities over $100K" → calls salesforce_soql()
💻

Code Execution Skill

Run Python, JavaScript, or bash scripts to perform calculations, data analysis, or generate files.

Example: "Calculate churn rate from this CSV" → calls python_execute()
🗂️

Document Skill

Create, read, and edit Word/PDF/Excel/Google Docs. Extract structured data from unstructured documents.

Example: "Create a BRD from this transcript" → calls docx_create()
🌐

API Skill

Call any external REST API — Jira, Slack, payment processors, ERPs. Connects AI to any web service.

Example: "Create a Jira story for this feature" → calls jira_create_issue()

How a Skill Works Internally — Function Calling Flow

1

User Intent Detected

Agent receives: "What's the weather in Chicago?" — the LLM recognizes this needs live data, not a training-based answer.

2

LLM Generates a Tool Call (JSON)

Instead of answering in text, the LLM outputs: {"tool": "weather_api", "params": {"city": "Chicago"}}. The host application intercepts this.

3

Host Executes the Skill

Your application (not the LLM) calls the actual weather API. Gets back real data: {"temp": "72°F", "condition": "Partly cloudy"}.

4

Result Fed Back to LLM

The tool result is added to the conversation as a "tool result" message. The LLM now generates a natural language response: "It's currently 72°F and partly cloudy in Chicago."

Section 07

AI Agents — AI That Plans and Acts

An AI agent is an LLM that doesn't just respond — it reasons through multi-step goals, selects and uses tools (skills), observes results, and loops until the task is complete — with minimal human intervention at each step.

🤖 Analogy: A chatbot is a vending machine — press a button, get one item. An agent is like hiring a capable employee — you say "research our top 5 competitors and email me a report by 5pm," and it figures out the steps, does the work, and delivers. You check the final result, not every micro-step.

The Agent Reasoning Loop — ReAct Pattern

👤

Human Gives a Goal

"Research the top 3 Salesforce competitors, compare their pricing, and create a Google Doc summary."

🧠

Agent THINKS — Plans Subtasks

"I need to: 1) identify top Salesforce competitors, 2) search each for pricing, 3) compare them side-by-side, 4) create a Google Doc with the comparison." Planning complete.

🔧

Agent ACTS — Calls a Skill

Calls web_search("top Salesforce CRM competitors 2025") — gets back structured results: HubSpot, Microsoft Dynamics, Zoho CRM.

👁️

Agent OBSERVES — Reads Results

Reads the search results. Realizes it needs pricing for each. Updates its internal plan: "Now search pricing for HubSpot, then Dynamics, then Zoho."

🔁

LOOP — Repeat Until Goal is Met

Runs 3 more searches. Synthesizes all data. Calls gdocs_create(content). Sends the doc link back to the user. Task complete — autonomously.

AI Co-workers & Multi-Agent Systems

An AI co-worker is an agent designed to work persistently alongside a human team. Multi-agent systems are several specialized agents collaborating — like a team of AI specialists, each handling what they're best at, coordinated by an orchestrator.

👥 Analogy: Think of a consulting engagement: a Project Manager (Orchestrator) coordinates a Researcher, a Lawyer, and a Financial Analyst — each a specialist. The PM delegates tasks, collects deliverables, and presents the final result. That's a multi-agent system. Each "agent" is an LLM with specialized skills, prompt, and data access.

MCP — Model Context Protocol

MCP is an open standard created by Anthropic that defines a universal way for AI agents to connect to external tools, data sources, and services — regardless of which AI model or which tool vendor is involved.

🔌 USB Analogy: Before USB, every device had a different connector — printers, cameras, keyboards all used different cables. USB standardized it: one port, any device. MCP does the same for AI. You build an MCP server for Salesforce once — and any AI (Claude, GPT, Gemini) can use it with zero custom integration work.

Section 10

Prompt Engineering — Talking to AI Right

A prompt is your instruction to an AI. How you write it dramatically changes output quality. Prompt engineering is the skill of communicating clearly and precisely with AI to get consistently great results.

❌ Vague Prompt
write something about salesforce
No role, no context, no format, no goal. You'll get a generic, shallow paragraph that's useful to no one.
✅ Precise Prompt
You are a Salesforce expert. Write a 200-word explanation of Salesforce CPQ for a non-technical VP of Sales. Avoid jargon. Focus on business value and ROI. Use bullet points for key benefits.
Role, length, audience, tone, format, focus — all specified. The output will be on-target every time.

Advanced Prompting Techniques

Chain of Thought
"Think step by step"
Ask the model to reason through a problem before answering. Dramatically improves accuracy on complex reasoning tasks. Add "Let's think through this step by step" to any hard question.
Few-shot Prompting
Show examples
Give 2–3 input/output examples before your actual request. The model patterns its response after your examples. Best for consistent formatting, custom styles, or specific output schemas.
Role Prompting
Set a persona
"You are a senior contract lawyer at a Fortune 500 company..." The model adopts that expertise and frames every answer through that lens. Elevates response quality significantly.
Self-Consistency
Ask multiple times
For critical decisions, ask the same question 3 times with temperature > 0. Compare answers. The one appearing most often is usually most reliable. Used in production agent pipelines.

Section 12

A2A — Agent-to-Agent Protocol

A2A is an emerging open standard (led by Google, supported by 50+ companies) that allows AI agents from different vendors and platforms to communicate, delegate tasks, and collaborate as peers — even when built on completely different AI models.

📧 Email Analogy: Email is a protocol letting Gmail users talk to Outlook users and Yahoo users — different services, one standard. A2A does the same for AI agents: a Salesforce Agentforce agent can delegate a research task to a Google Vertex AI agent, and they communicate via A2A — neither cares what model the other runs on.
MCP — Vertical Connection
Agent ↔ Tool
How an intelligent agent connects to external tools and data sources. The agent calls the tool. One-directional intelligence — only the agent thinks. Tools are passive.

Analogy: A chef (agent) using kitchen appliances (tools). The oven doesn't have opinions.
A2A — Horizontal Connection
Agent ↔ Agent
How peer agents communicate and negotiate tasks. Both sides are intelligent. They can ask clarifying questions, refuse tasks, propose alternatives.

Analogy: Two expert consultants working together — both bring expertise, both communicate as equals.
Real Multi-vendor A2A Example: Your company's Salesforce Agentforce agent handles a complex customer renewal. It realizes it needs contract analysis → delegates via A2A to an Ironclad CLM Agent → which requests pricing data via A2A from a Zuora billing agent → all three collaborate and return a complete renewal package. Three agents, three vendors, one A2A protocol.

Section 14

Complete Glossary

Every key term — click to expand. Bookmark this section for quick reference.

TokenThe basic unit of text AI processes▾
A token is roughly 3–4 characters or ¾ of a word. "Hello world" = 2 tokens. "Anthropic" = 3 tokens. LLMs don't see letters — they see tokens (numbers). Context windows are measured in tokens: Claude's 200K token window ≈ 150,000 words or ~300 pages. More tokens = more context but higher API cost.
Embeddings / VectorsHow AI measures semantic meaning numerically▾
An embedding converts text into a list of ~1500 floating-point numbers (a vector) that represents its meaning. Texts with similar meanings produce numerically similar vectors. "Cat" and "kitten" → vectors close together. "Cat" and "skyscraper" → vectors far apart. Cosine similarity measures closeness. The foundation of semantic search and RAG.
Context WindowThe AI's active short-term memory▾
The context window is the total text the model can "see" at once — the conversation history, system prompt, retrieved chunks, and tool results all count against it. GPT-4o: 128K tokens. Claude 3.7: 200K. Gemini 1.5 Pro: 1M. Everything outside the window is forgotten. RAG solves this by injecting only the most relevant chunks rather than entire documents.
HallucinationWhen AI confidently states things that are false▾
Hallucination happens when an LLM generates confident-sounding text that is factually wrong. It occurs because the model is predicting statistically likely text — not necessarily true text. A model might confidently cite a paper that doesn't exist. Solutions: RAG (ground answers in real documents), system prompts instructing "say I don't know if uncertain," retrieval verification layers.
TemperatureControls creativity vs. consistency▾
Temperature (0.0–2.0) controls how random the model's word selection is. At 0: always picks the statistically most likely next token — deterministic, consistent. Good for Q&A and factual analysis. At 1.0: creative variation, explores less likely options. Good for brainstorming and creative writing. Agents doing data analysis: use 0. Content generation: 0.7–1.0.
System PromptHidden instructions that define every AI product▾
The system prompt is a hidden instruction block given to the model before the user speaks. It defines persona, rules, tone, available tools, restrictions, and output format. Every product built on LLMs (Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Copilot) has a system prompt. Example: "You are a Salesforce CPQ specialist. Only answer CPQ questions. Cite sources. Never guess pricing. Say 'I don't know' if uncertain."
Function Calling / Tool UseHow the LLM reaches into the real world▾
Instead of generating text, the LLM can output a structured JSON "tool call" — specifying which skill to invoke and with what parameters. The host application (not the LLM) actually executes the function, gets real data back, and returns it to the LLM as a "tool result." The LLM then incorporates real data into its final answer. This is the bridge between language model and real-world action.
Generative AI vs Agentic AIPassive responder vs. autonomous actor▾
Generative AI creates content when you prompt it — one turn, one output. You do the work of assembling the pieces. Agentic AI acts: it plans multi-step workflows, uses tools, makes decisions, loops until tasks are complete — minimal human steering needed at each step. Today's frontier: moving from "ask and get an answer" to "assign a goal and get it done."
RLHFReinforcement Learning from Human Feedback▾
RLHF is the training technique that turns a base language model into a helpful, safe assistant. Human raters compare pairs of model responses and indicate which is better. A "reward model" learns to score responses. The LLM is then fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize the reward score. This is why Claude is polite, follows instructions, and refuses harmful requests — it was trained to be preferred by humans.
Semantic Search vs Keyword SearchMeaning vs. exact words▾
Keyword search finds documents containing the exact words you typed. Semantic search finds documents with similar meaning, even if they use different words. "How do I log in?" semantically matches "account authentication procedure" even without shared keywords. RAG uses semantic search via vector embeddings so users get relevant results regardless of exact phrasing. Far more natural and robust than keyword search.
Inference vs TrainingUsing a model vs. building a model▾
Training is the expensive, one-time (or periodic) process of updating a model's weights on a large dataset — requires thousands of GPUs, weeks, and millions of dollars. Inference is using a trained model to generate responses — runs on much less hardware, milliseconds per request, and is what happens every time you send a message to Claude. API pricing is for inference, not training.

RAG Chunking StrategyHow documents are split for optimal retrieval▾
How you chunk documents dramatically impacts RAG quality. Fixed-size chunking: split every N tokens regardless of content (simple but breaks sentences). Semantic chunking: split at natural paragraph or section boundaries (better coherence). Hierarchical chunking: store both chunk-level and document-level summaries (enables both specific and broad retrieval). Overlap: chunks overlap by 20% so context isn't lost at boundaries. The right strategy depends on your document type and query patterns.
